How many experiments?

You want to know which of your compounds (volatile organic compounds, hormones, nutrients…) act on their own and which act only in combination. Testing every pair or triplet separately quickly becomes impossible. A combinatorial design (D. Shasha) mixes many compounds in each experiment, so that every pair or triplet is seen in all its presence / absence situations. This calculator tells you how many mixtures you need to find all the effects with 95 % confidence. Everything runs in your browser.

How it works

Combinatorial design. With K compounds there are K(K−1)/2 pairs and K(K−1)(K−2)/6 triplets (153 and 816 for 18 compounds). Instead of one experiment per combination, each mixture contains many compounds, chosen so that every pair (or triplet) appears in all its situations: none present, each one alone, both (all three) together. This is the approach of Dennis Shasha's CombGeneric, first applied to plant signalling by Shasha et al. (2001), itself inspired by the AETG method of software testing.

Pairs. The smallest design is known exactly (Kleitman & Spencer 1973): the first mixture has no compound, and each compound is present in a different set of ⌈N/2⌉ of the other mixtures. 18 compounds need 8 mixtures, 50 need 9. Triplets have no closed solution; the page searches one by a greedy construction followed by simulated annealing (18 compounds: 20 mixtures).

Why more mixtures than the minimal design. The minimal designs show each combination, but with 18 compounds and only 8 or 20 mixtures, the effects cannot be told apart once many compounds are active: there are more unknowns than mixtures. The wave plan adds the foldover of the triplet design (every present compound becomes absent and vice versa), which separates the simple effects from the combined ones, then copies with the compounds permuted. Run the waves one after the other: predict each new wave with the model of the previous ones; when the prediction error is close to the replicate noise, the result is solid.

Simulations. For each number of mixtures, experiments are simulated: a random balanced design (each compound in half of the mixtures), S compounds with a simple effect, M combined effects, noise, replicates. The analysis estimates the simple effect of every compound, then adds combined effects one at a time among all candidates (all pairs, or all pairs and triplets) while the best one is significant after Bonferroni correction (family-wise risk 1 %). A simulation succeeds when every true simple effect is significant (5 %) and exactly the true combined effects are kept. N95 is where the logistic fit of the success rate reaches 95 %.

Formulas. Fitted on 54 (pairs) and 42 (triplets) combinations of S and M, 200 simulations each, for 18 compounds, 3 replicates, noise SD 1, simple effects 0.8–3 and combined effects 2.5–5:

pairs:  N95 ≈ 35 + 0.45 S + 6.4 M2   (R² = 0.996)
triplets:  N95 ≈ 39 + 0.15 S + 7.9 M3 + 0.9 M3²   (R² = 0.996)

About 35–40 mixtures are needed to estimate the 18 simple effects and separate them from noise; each simple effect then costs almost nothing, each pairwise effect about 6 mixtures, and triplet effects cost more and more (≈ 9 for the first, ≈ 1.8 more for each next one): a triplet is complete in only 1/8 of the mixtures (1/4 for a pair) and is searched among 969 candidates instead of 153. On configurations not used for the fit, the formula gives 91–94 %: the page adds a 10 % margin. Halving the combined effects multiplies N by ≈ 1.6; one replicate instead of three by ≈ 2.3 (but uses fewer tubes). For other settings, use Simulate my situation.
